Collusion audits test whether AI agents' actions depend on each other, but agents running on one model (a monoculture) act alike without communicating. With four honest Claude Haiku agents in rock-paper-scissors, a mutual-information test accuses a pair in 69.3% of 30-round games; it also flags 8 of 10 published auction runs where sellers could not talk. Our replay test asks each deployed agent again, every round, on what it saw minus its private channel, and flags a pair only if their moves depend on each other more than their replays do. If replayed calls are served like the originals, its 5% false-alarm promise holds by construction within each round; a diagnostic is consistent with this for the main models, and over whole games we measure it: 4.2% of 550 honest LLM games, with the analysis fixed in advance. In a procurement auction it names the pair in 92.5% of games where a pricing vendor shares two clients' costs, as alleged in Duffy v. Yardi, and flags 1.7% when the vendor uses only public data. In rock-paper-scissors it names 35% of Haiku and 10% of DeepSeek pairs instructed to collude, depending on how strongly the channel shapes moves. A sequential version that can stop early put the instructed pair first in 18/20 games against 11/20 for the fixed-length test in a pre-registered replication. Coordination written into shared instructions is invisible to it by design.
