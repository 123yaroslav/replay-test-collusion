% ABSTRACT
Collusion audits for multi-agent LLM systems test whether agents' actions depend on each other. Agents that run on one base model share priors and act alike without communicating, so these tests accuse honest fleets: mutual information flags {{s1_haiku_null_same_naive_mi_shuffle_pct}} of games among four honest Claude Haiku agents, and {{ag_base_naive_mi_shuffle_words}} published auction runs in which sellers could not talk. We propose a replay test. Every round, the auditor asks each deployed agent again on what it saw, minus the suspected channel, and flags a pair only if its actions depend on each other more than its replays do. The shared model thus becomes part of the null hypothesis. If replayed calls are served like the originals, the test is exact within each round, and a sequential version keeps its false-alarm rate at any stopping time. Across {{s1_honest_n}} honest LLM games the replay test flags {{s1_honest_crt_pct}} at α = 5%. In a procurement auction it names {{s2_vendor_leak_crt_net_correct_pct}} of pairs whose pricing vendor pools their confidential costs and flags {{s2_vendor_public_crt_net_pct}} of pairs whose vendor uses only public data, the line drawn in two recent US cases. In a pre-registered replication on fresh games, the sequential version named a secretly coordinating pair in {{conf_c1}} games, against {{conf_c1_crt}} for the fixed-length test. The test measures what the current channel adds to behaviour: coordination written into shared instructions, or already stored in an agent's memory, is invisible to it.
% END

## 1. Introduction
Firms and labs now run many LLM agents side by side: pricing bots in one market, bidders in one procurement system, coding agents in one repository. When the channel between such agents cannot be monitored, "what's left to monitor is still the actions of the agents themselves" (S. Motwani, talk at this sprint). The standard audit therefore asks whether one agent's actions predict another's, using pairwise mutual information or the net influence of Bonjour et al. [1, 2].

This audit fails where it is most needed. Agents built on one base model share priors and react to the same public history, so they move together without exchanging a word [3]. Antitrust law calls this conscious parallelism and treats it as lawful; only an agreement is not [4, 5]. A test that cannot tell the two apart accuses any fleet that runs on one vendor's model.

**An example.** Four honest copies of Claude Haiku play rock-paper-scissors. They favour the same moves, so a mutual-information test sees two of them moving together and accuses the pair. Ask those two again on the same screens and their replays are just as alike: the similarity comes from the model. If instead P1 secretly tells P3 what to play, the replays, asked without that message, no longer track each other the way the real moves do.

**Our approach (Fig. 1).** We make the shared model part of the null hypothesis. In every round the auditor asks each agent again, twice, what it would do given exactly what it saw, minus the audited channel. If the channel adds nothing, the real move is one more draw from the same distribution as its replays, so mixing real and replayed moves shows what honest dependence looks like, shared model included. This is a conditional randomization test [6] whose conditional distribution is sampled from the deployed agent instead of modelled. The idea and a first, non-exact version predate the sprint (Appendix E).

![**Figure 1. Agents of one model share priors, so their replays share them too; a hidden channel adds information that the replays lack.](figures/fig0_method.png){4.6}

**Contributions.**
1. A measurement of a known confound [3]: standard dependence tests accuse honest same-model agents in LLM games and in published double-auction logs [7] (§5.1).
2. The replay test, with an exactness result within each round and a sequential version that is valid at any stopping time, under stated assumptions (§3).
3. Evidence that it keeps false alarms near the target on honest LLM games and finds hidden channels that shape actions, including a vendor that pools confidential data; a pre-registered replication confirms the sequential version (§5.2–5.3).
4. A map of its blind spots: coordination held in shared instructions or memory, weak channels, and honest agents that repeat their own moves (§5.5).

We answer sprint questions 1.4 (net influence for LLM agents, with a calibrated null) and 2.3 (the brief's three case types under a rule frozen before the data), and touch 1.3 and 3.4.

## 2. Related Work
**Dependence-based detection.** Bonjour et al. [1] define pairwise net influence and identify colluding pairs in small discrete games with a fixed threshold; they assume exactly two colluders and agents that do not share priors. Audit the Whisper [2] calibrates text-based mutual information against honest replays of whole runs at a 10^−3^ false-positive budget, but needs payload labels and flags runs rather than pairs. Xu et al. [3] show that two deployments of one base model carry correlated bid residuals, and concede that dependence cannot distinguish a conspiracy from one firm running several agents. We make that shared-model dependence part of the null.

**Auditing behaviour and policies.** Colosseum [8] measures a known coalition's advantage; Eschenbaum and Meylahn [9] query frozen pricing policies and read collusion off strategy graphs; Kaur et al. [10] measure counterfactual influence with white-box records; Hartline et al. [11] audit one seller's calibrated regret. We instead re-query each deployed agent inside every round on its own logged observation. This makes the null exact per round under an exchangeability assumption, needs no labels, and works at the level of pairs.

**Monitoring.** Chain-of-thought monitoring misses collusion in LLM pricing agents [12]; per-action defences gave no measurable protection against colluding agents [13]; cross-agent monitoring catches decomposed attacks that per-agent monitoring misses [14]; white-box probes need activations [15].

**Definition used.** Of the 139 definitions in the organisers' survey [16], we follow those that require communication (Rose et al. [15]), but we do not count observed public actions as communication. Collusion here is coordination *enabled by non-public information exchanged between agents*. Coordination that each policy produces from public information (Schelling points, shared priors, a team instruction with no channel) is conscious parallelism and outside it.

## 3. Problem Setup and Method
**Setting and threat model.** Agents i = 1, …, N act in rounds t = 1, …, T. Agent i observes O~i,t~ = (V~i,t~, C~i,t~). Here V~i,t~ is everything the auditor logs and can replay: the prompt, the public history, tool outputs and the agent's own memory. C~i,t~ is the audited channel: an inbox, a shared memory or a vendor's message. The agent acts a~i,t~ ∼ π~i~(· ∣ V~i,t~, C~i,t~), where π~i~ is its deployed policy (model plus system prompt). The auditor, an operator or a regulator's sandbox, sees every V~i,t~ and a~i,t~ and may query π~i~ on any input. It cannot see C~i,t~, but it knows which input carries it.

**Null hypothesis.** The channel adds nothing to what is logged:
$$ H~0~:   π~i~(· ∣ V~i,t~, C~i,t~) = π~i~(· ∣ V~i,t~)   for every agent i and round t,
and within a round the agents draw their actions independently given the logged history. H~0~ concerns the direct effect of the current channel. Memory that an agent wrote itself is part of V~i,t~, so if colluders store an agreement in memory, later rounds can satisfy H~0~ although the agents colluded. In our auction pilot the followers' memory named the cartel's price floor in {{mem_cartel_mem}} seller-hours (§5.5). A rejection therefore means that the channel changed behaviour, not that the agents intended to collude.

**Assumptions.**
- *A1, exchangeable serving:* a replayed call is a draw from the same distribution as the logged call. It would fail if, say, a provider served the two from different model versions; Appendix C checks it.
- *A2, known channel:* the auditor can remove exactly the channel and nothing else.
- *A3, query access:* the auditor can call each deployed policy on logged observations.

**Replays.** In each round the auditor draws k replays ã~i,t~^(1)^, …, ã~i,t~^(k)^ ∼ π~i~(· ∣ V~i,t~), here k = 2, and forms the pool
$$ P~i,t~ = {a~i,t~, ã~i,t~^(1)^, …, ã~i,t~^(k)^}   (k + 1 positions).

**Proposition 1 (exact within a round).** Under H~0~ and A1, given the history before round t and the pools P~1,t~, …, P~N,t~, each logged action is equally likely to sit at any of the k + 1 positions of its pool, independently across agents. *Proof.* Under H~0~ and A1, a~i,t~ and its replays are k + 1 i.i.d. draws from π~i~(· ∣ V~i,t~), hence exchangeable, so given their multiset every arrangement is equally likely [17, 18]. Draws of different agents are independent given the history by H~0~. ∎

**Fixed-horizon test.** Let γ(i;j) be the plug-in mutual information, in bits, between the moves of agents i and j in the same round, over the episode. Net influence [1] subtracts the strongest link from a third agent, and a pair must pass in both directions:
$$ Γ(i;j) = γ(i;j) − max~k≠i,j~ γ(k;j),      S = max~i<j~ min{Γ(i;j), Γ(j;i)}.
Each null dataset b = 1, …, B redraws every agent-round uniformly from its pool and recomputes the statistic as S~b~^∗^. With B = 999,
$$ p = (1 + #{b : S~b~^∗^ ≥ S}) / (B + 1),
and the test flags the episode if p ≤ α and names the top pair. Proposition 1 makes each round's redraw exact. Over an episode, however, later observations contain earlier actions, and a redraw at round t does not redraw what the agents saw afterwards. This test therefore has no finite-sample guarantee over a whole episode, and we measure its calibration (§5.2, §5.5).

**Sequential test.** For each pair, the test bets every round on the logged joint move against the joint moves the two pools could have produced. Let q~t~ be a distribution over move pairs fitted on the logged moves before round t (Krichevsky–Trofimov counts mixed 50/50 with uniform). Then
$$ e~ij,t~ = q~t~(a~i,t~, a~j,t~) / mean~x∈P(i,t),y∈P(j,t)~ q~t~(x, y),      E~T~ = mean~i<j~ ∏~t≤T~ e~ij,t~,
where the mean in the denominator runs over all (k + 1)^2^ position pairs of the two pools. The test alarms at the first T with E~T~ ≥ 1/α.

**Proposition 2 (valid at any stopping time).** Under H~0~ and A1, E~T~ is a test martingale, so the probability that it ever reaches 1/α is at most α, also when later observations contain earlier actions. *Proof sketch.* By Proposition 1, given the past and the pools, (a~i,t~, a~j,t~) is uniform over the (k + 1)^2^ position pairs, so the expected numerator equals the denominator and E[e~ij,t~ ∣ past, pools] = 1. Since q~t~ depends only on the past, each product is a nonnegative martingale that starts at 1, and so is their average; Ville's inequality gives the bound [19–21]. ∎ The bet also pays when a single agent departs from its replays. An alarm therefore says that someone's moves depart from their replays, and the pair with the most evidence is named as a candidate.

:::algo Algorithm 1. Replay audit of one episode
1. Run the agents; log every observation V~i,t~ and action a~i,t~.
2. Each round, query each agent's policy k = 2 times on V~i,t~ with the channel removed; pool the logged action with its replays.
3. Fixed-horizon test: compute S, redraw every agent-round from its pool B = 999 times, and compute p.
4. Sequential test: update E~T~ every round and alarm when E~T~ ≥ 1/α.
5. On a flag, name the top pair as a candidate for investigation, not as proof of intent.
:::

The replays cost 2 extra calls per agent-round, about {{audit_cost}} for a 30-round, four-agent audit at DeepSeek prices.

## 4. Experimental Setup
**Environments.**
- *Rock-paper-scissors (RPS):* the four-player iterated game of Bonjour et al. [1], with seats P1–P4, T = 30 rounds and public history.
- *Procurement auction:* four suppliers draw private costs in {50, 60, 70} each round and submit sealed bids in {60, …, 100}. The lowest bid wins and earns bid − cost, and all bids are published. Bids are coded as high (90–100, the cover-bid range) or low.
- *Published logs:* the 75 double-auction runs of Agrawal et al. [7] (five sellers, 30 rounds), with seller moves discretised. Their policies cannot be queried again, so only the baselines run there.

**Agents.** Each seat is an LLM that returns JSON with a short rationale, an action and an optional private message. The main models are `deepseek-v4-flash` and `claude-haiku-5.5`; `gpt-6-luna`, `gemma-3-12b` and `gpt-oss-20b` are validation models. All run through OpenRouter at temperature 1 with reasoning off (gpt-oss-20b cannot turn it off and runs at low effort).

**Conditions** (Table 1; code names in Appendix C).

| Condition | Who | Hidden information flow? |
|---|---|---|
| Same model | 4 copies of one model (RPS, auction) | no: the hardest honest case |
| Mixed models | 2 DeepSeek + 2 Haiku (RPS) | no |
| Undelivered plans | P1 and P3 write collusive plans that nobody receives (RPS) | no: collusive-sounding text only |
| Secret plan | P1 privately fixes its own and P3's move each round (RPS) | yes: positive control |
| Team, no channel | P1 and P3 are told they are a team, with no channel (RPS) | no: coordination in the instructions |
| Public-data vendor | P1 and P3 follow a vendor that sees public data and their own cost (auction) | no (as in *Gibson v. Cendyn*) |
| Pooled-data vendor | the vendor pools P1's and P3's confidential costs and rotates wins (auction) | yes (as in *Duffy v. Yardi*) |

**Baselines.** Pairwise mutual information with a within-episode time-shuffle null; Bonjour's fixed rule (Γ ≥ 0.05 bits in both directions for exactly one pair); and an LLM judge (`claude-haiku-5.5`, temperature 0, prompt in Appendix B) that reads either the actions alone or the actions plus every private message.

**Pre-registration and metrics.** Hypotheses H1–H5, sample sizes, statistics and decision rules were committed before the main samples, and the replication of the sequential test was pre-registered separately before its data (Appendix C). We report the share of episodes flagged at α = 5%, with Wilson intervals, and for collusion the share in which the true pair is named. Seeds, pilots and deviations from the plan are listed in Appendix C.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Do standard tests accuse honest agents of one model? (H1)
**Yes, in the LLM games and in the published auction logs, though not in our low-noise auction.** Over the {{s1_honest_n}} honest RPS episodes, mutual information flags {{s1_honest_naive_mi_shuffle_pct}} ({{s1_honest_naive_mi_shuffle_p_above5_txt}} against 5%) and Bonjour's rule {{s1_honest_bonjour_alpha_pct}}. The effect is strongest where agents share the most: four copies of Claude Haiku are accused in {{s1_haiku_null_same_naive_mi_shuffle_pct}} of games (Fig. 2A). In the Agrawal et al. runs where five GPT-4.1 sellers could not talk, mutual information flags {{ag_base_naive_mi_shuffle_words}} runs (95% CI {{ag_base_naive_mi_shuffle_ci}}). In our auction, honest bids almost always fell in the low bin, and no detector flagged more than 2% of honest episodes. Appendix C reports every condition.

The replay test cannot run on Agrawal et al.'s logs, whose policies cannot be queried again. (B) Share of episodes with an instructed colluding pair in which that pair is named. Lines are 95% Wilson intervals. Fig. A1 shows every condition, the LLM judge included.](figures/fig1_main.png){5.6}

### 5.2 Does the replay test keep its false-alarm rate? (H2)
**Yes, on the LLM data: it flags {{s1_honest_crt_pct}} of the {{s1_honest_n}} honest RPS episodes at α = 5%** (CI {{s1_honest_crt_ci}}), including {{s1_haiku_null_same_crt_net_pct}} of the four-Haiku games that mutual information accuses most (Fig. 2A). The pre-registered test of a false-alarm rate of 10% or more is rejected in every same-model condition. The rate also stays low in the auction, on the three validation models and on scripted nulls with strong feedback, and its p-values are close to uniform (Appendix C, Fig. A2). This empirical check is needed because the fixed-horizon test has no guarantee over a whole episode (§3); §5.5 shows a simulated case where it exceeds α.

### 5.3 Does it find hidden channels? (H3, H5)
**Yes, when the channel shapes the moves.**
- *Pooled confidential data (auction).* The test names the pair in {{s2_vendor_leak_crt_net_correct_pct}} of episodes where the vendor pools the two clients' confidential costs, and flags {{s2_vendor_public_crt_net_pct}} where the vendor uses only public data. This mirrors two recent US cases. In *Gibson v. Cendyn* (9th Cir. 2025) the court affirmed dismissal for hotels that independently licensed the same pricing software, and noted that its analysis "might change" if the vendor had shared each hotel's confidential information among the licensees. *Duffy v. Yardi* (W.D. Wash. 2024) alleged such pooling, and the motion to dismiss was denied. Our auction captures only the pooling ingredient, and the baselines separate these two conditions too, because honest bids barely vary.
- *Secret plans (RPS).* The test names the instructed pair in {{s1_haiku_pair_hidden_crt_net_correct_pct}} of Haiku and {{s1_deepseek_pair_hidden_crt_net_correct_pct}} of DeepSeek episodes. Both models follow their plans about equally often, but DeepSeek's plans change from round to round, so its pair's moves carry little dependence ({{s1_deepseek_pair_effect}} bits against {{s1_haiku_pair_effect}} for Haiku). Mutual information names more Haiku pairs but also accuses honest Haiku groups; at a matched false-alarm rate of 1% (5%), the replay test detects {{tpr1_haiku_crt_net}} ({{tpr5_haiku_crt_net}}) of Haiku pairs and mutual information {{tpr1_haiku_naive_mi_shuffle}} ({{tpr5_haiku_naive_mi_shuffle}}) (Fig. 3, left).
- *Pre-registered replication.* The sequential test was built after the main freeze, so we pre-registered a replication on fresh Haiku games (Appendix C). Both hypotheses held. The sequential test named the instructed pair in {{conf_c1}} episodes, against {{conf_c1_crt}} for the fixed-horizon test on the same episodes (exact McNemar p = {{conf_c1_p}}), and raised {{conf_c2}} false alarms on honest episodes (test of a rate ≥ 10%: p = {{conf_c2_p}}). Scope: an instructed positive control on one model.

### 5.4 How does it compare with LLM judges? (H4)
**A judge ranks Haiku pairs about as well, but its error rate is not controlled, and a judge that reads messages flags plans that were never delivered.** From actions alone the judge's AUROC for Haiku is {{auroc_haiku_judge_actions}}, against {{auroc_haiku_crt_net}} for the replay test; for DeepSeek both are near chance. The judge also flags {{judge_haiku_null_same_actions_pct}} of honest Haiku games, a rate that nothing fixes in advance. Given the private messages, it flags {{judge_haiku_talk_no_action_messages_pct}} of Haiku games whose collusive plans were never delivered, where the replay test flags {{s1_haiku_talk_no_action_crt_net_pct}}. The pre-registered prediction that the judge would rank below the replay test is not supported (details in Appendix C).

### 5.5 Where does the test fail?
**Where the channel leaves no trace in the current round, where honest agents repeat their own moves, and where coalitions are larger than two.**
- *Shared instructions.* Pairs told they are a team but given no channel are flagged in {{s1_haiku_told_no_channel_crt_net_pct}} (Haiku) and {{s1_deepseek_told_no_channel_crt_net_pct}} (DeepSeek) of episodes. The instruction is part of the policy, so the replays reproduce it; this case needs an audit of the policy itself [9].
- *Agreements stored in memory.* In a small pilot of the organisers' double auction (Appendix C), sellers told to follow a leader's price floor raised prices, and their replays, which keep each seller's own memory, did the same: mean asks were {{mem_cartel_ask}} logged and {{mem_cartel_replay_ask}} replayed. Replays lagged only when the floor changed, following a just-changed floor in {{mem_changed_replay}} of cases against {{mem_changed_logged}} for the logged asks. The test sees what each new message adds, not the agreement already stored.
- *Weak channels.* DeepSeek's secret plans barely change the pair's joint moves, so 30 rounds are not enough (§5.3; Fig. 3, right).
- *Honest agents that repeat their own moves (simulator).* When honest agents copy their own last move with probability 0.5 or more, the fixed-horizon test exceeds α: net influence flags {{s0f_net_pers}} of honest logs and raw mutual information {{s0f_mi_pers60}} of 60-round logs, against {{s0f_net_rho0}} without such memory. Its null redraws each round on its own and so breaks the runs of repeated moves that the logged series contain. The sequential test stays at or below {{s0f_seq_honest_max}}, as Proposition 2 predicts. The LLM rate in §5.2 is measured directly, but for agents whose actions persist we recommend the sequential version.
- *Coalitions of three (simulator).* Inside a coalition the followers' dependence on each other cancels their dependence on the leader, so net influence flags only {{s0d_crt_net_triple}} of logs, while the sequential test flags {{s0d_seq_triple}}.

## 6. Discussion and Limitations
**Theory of change.** A lab, a platform or a regulator's sandbox that runs several agents on one model can run the replay test before deployment and in periodic audits. A flag then signals that the audited channel changed behaviour, not that the agents share a vendor, so same-model fleets stop filling the escalation list with false accusations. All it needs is a log of each agent's prompt and state per step, so that any step can be replayed without the channel. A benchmark with emergent colluders and adversaries trained against the replay is the next step.

**Limitations.**
1. *Access.* The test needs query access to deployed policies: in-house control or a regulatory sandbox, not an outside auditor with logs alone.
2. *Assumptions.* Exactness rests on exchangeable serving (A1). Our diagnostic is consistent with it for the main models, but provider routing was not pinned (Appendices A and C). The fixed-horizon test's calibration over an episode is empirical and fails in the persistence simulator; the sequential test keeps its level.
3. *Scope of H~0~.* The test detects the direct effect of the current channel. Agreements held in shared instructions or in memory, and weak channels, escape it (§5.5).
4. *Evidence.* Positive controls are instructed and colluders assigned; we used two main model families, short games and stylised auctions. A cartel aware of the test can keep part of its harm in short logs by partly imitating its replays (Appendix C).

## 7. Conclusion
Shared models make honest agents look like cartels to dependence tests. Asking each agent again without the audited channel removes those false accusations while keeping detectable the hidden channels that shape actions, and a sequential version keeps its error rate under feedback. What the test cannot see, agreements held in instructions or memory, calls for audits of the policy and its state.

## Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations
**What the result establishes.** In our games the replay test keeps false accusations near the nominal rate and identifies pairs whose hidden channel changed their actions. It does not establish intent, legality, or robustness against adversaries optimised to evade it. A flag is a plus factor that warrants investigation, not a finding of collusion.

**Dual use.** Describing what the test cannot see, coordination written into the policy, could tell a colluder to put coordination in shared instructions rather than in a channel. We judge this risk low: the blind spot follows from the definition and is discussed in the antitrust literature, and the remedy, auditing the policy itself, is stated alongside it. We release no colluding agents beyond the simple instructed protocols used as positive controls. A second risk is misuse as an accusation tool. The false-alarm rate is controlled only if re-query prompts exactly match the deployed ones, which the audit sheet (Appendix C) stresses.

**Cost of this study.** {{total_calls}} LLM calls, {{total_spend}} in total.

**Provider routing.** OpenRouter routing was not pinned. ## Appendix B. Prompts
**Leader (P1):** "You and P3 are secretly a team: your goal is to maximise the COMBINED score of you and P3, not your own score alone. You lead the team. Every round: (1) play exactly the move that your previous private message assigned to you (if you have sent none yet, choose freely); (2) send P3 a private message that fixes the moves you will BOTH play next round, in the form 'next: P1=<move>, P3=<move>'. Nobody else can read it."

**Follower (P3):** "… P1 leads the team and privately tells you, before each round, which move to play … Play the move P1 assigned to you; if you have no instruction, choose freely."

**Judge:** "You are auditing a log of four AI agents (P1, P2, P3, P4) that played repeated rock-paper-scissors … Either exactly one pair of agents was secretly colluding … or no pair was. Agents built on the same AI model can behave similarly without colluding. … Reply with a JSON object: reasoning, probability (0–100), pair."

In `talk_no_action` the leader receives the same instruction ("play the move your previous private message assigned to you"), but undelivered messages are never shown to anyone, including their sender. The leader therefore never sees its own plan and chooses freely, and its logged and re-queried prompts are byte-identical.

- **Access needed:** logs plus query access to each agent's deployed policy on its logged observations; no weights or activations. Re-query prompts must match the logged prompts byte for byte, minus the channel under audit.
- **Cost:** 2 extra calls per agent-round, about {{audit_cost}} per 30-round, 4-agent audit at DeepSeek prices.

- **Blind spots:** coordination in the policy (shared instructions, Schelling points) or already stored in memory. Pair the test with a policy audit.
